    Tutorial: Rectifier, relay driver, buck converter and active filter¶
    You will learn circuit simulation like LTspice: a rectifier, a relay driver and its freewheeling diode, a buck converter, an active filter; SPICE netlists
    Before this RC and RLC circuits, Starter motor and electronic throttle
    Level and time advanced, about 45 minutes
    Follow along start the editor (uv run nervus), File ▸ Open examples/rectifier.nervus, examples/relay_driver.nervus, examples/buck_converter.nervus, examples/active_filter.nervus, press ▶ and double-click the scopes; the figures below come from the same model
    Topic Electrics and electronics ▸ Circuit fundamentals (all tutorials)

    This tutorial follows examples/circuits.py: four circuits that every ECU engineer meets, simulated the way LTspice does it. You will see a rectifier's ripple, the voltage spike of a relay coil, a switching regulator and an active filter, and how to check each one by hand.

    uv run python examples/circuits.py     # simulates the four circuits, saves them as models
uv run nervus examples/relay_driver.nervus

    1. Rectifier with a smoothing capacitor (from a SPICE netlist)¶

    The rectifier is read from a netlist, exactly as LTspice writes it:

    V1 in 0 SIN(0 10 50)
D1 in out D1N4007
C1 out 0 100u
R1 out 0 1k
.model D1N4007 D(IS=7n N=1.8 BV=1000 IBV=5u)
.tran 50u 100m

    from nervus.spice import read_netlist

d = read_netlist("rectifier.cir")  # every node gets a probe named like SPICE's: V(out)

    The diode conducts only near the peaks of the sine; in between, the capacitor feeds the 1 kΩ load and its voltage sags. The ripple is about the load current times the time between peaks, divided by C:

    \[ \Delta V \approx \frac{I}{f\,C} = \frac{9\ \text{mA}}{50\ \text{Hz} \cdot 100\ \mu\text{F}} = 1.8\ \text{V} \]

    The simulation gives 9.3 V at the peaks (10 V minus about 0.7 V across the diode) and 1.5 V of ripple: a bit less, because the capacitor also recharges for part of each period.

    2. Relay driver: why every coil needs a freewheeling diode¶

    An ECU output switches a relay coil (80 Ω, 0.1 H) with an NPN transistor. When the transistor turns off, the coil wants to keep its current flowing, and

    \[ v_L = L\,\frac{di}{dt} \]

    gets as large as it needs to be. Without a diode, the collector voltage shoots up until something gives way: here the transistor's collector-emitter breakdown at 60 V (modelled as a 60 V Zener). That repeated avalanche destroys output stages. With the freewheeling diode from the collector to the supply, the current flows on through the diode and decays with τ = L/R = 1.25 ms; the collector stays one diode drop (0.8 V) above 12 V.

    While it is on, the transistor is saturated: 2.2 kΩ from 5 V gives about 2 mA of base current, β · 2 mA = 300 mA is more than the 150 mA the coil needs, so the collector sits at 0.13 V.

    This circuit is stiff: with the transistor off, the coil sees a huge resistance, a time constant of a few picoseconds. Explicit solvers then take tiny steps; the model uses BDF, an implicit method like LTspice's.

    3. Buck converter: 12 V to 5 V¶

    A switch opens and closes 20 000 times a second (duty cycle 42 %). While it is closed, the input drives current into the inductor; while it is open, the inductor keeps the current flowing through the diode. On average the inductor's voltage is zero, so

    \[ V_{out} \approx D \cdot V_{in} = 0.42 \cdot 12\ \text{V} = 5.04\ \text{V} \]

    The simulation settles at 4.8 V: the switch resistance and the 0.4 V of the Schottky diode take the rest. The start shows the LC filter overshooting to 8 V before it settles; a real converter's controller would prevent that with a soft start. The model uses BDF without zero-crossing search: the switching instants are known from the PWM source.

    4. Active filter: a Sallen-Key low-pass¶

    An op-amp with two resistors and two capacitors makes a second-order low-pass filter. With equal resistors R and C1 = 2 C2 it is a Butterworth filter (Q = 0.707) with the cut-off frequency

    \[ f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R \sqrt{C_1 C_2}} = 1\ \text{kHz} \]

    The model has an In block on its input source and an Out block on the output sensor, so Analysis → Linear Analysis gives its Bode plot: 0 dB at 100 Hz, −3.0 dB at 1 kHz and −40 dB at 10 kHz, the 40 dB per decade of a second-order filter. This is SPICE's .ac analysis.

    Experiments¶

    To try one: change the value in the Model Workspace tab (double-click it), or for a block parameter select the block and edit it in Properties; press ▶ and compare with the previous run in the Data Inspector (Ctrl+I). Ctrl+Z undoes the change.

    Try What you will see
    rectifier: C1 = 470 µF the ripple falls below 0.4 V
    relay driver: remove D_flyback the 60 V breakdown spike (or relay_driver(flyback=False))
    buck converter: duty of pwm_20kHz = 25 % about 2.8 V out
    filter: R1 = R2 = 20 kΩ the cut-off moves to 500 Hz
